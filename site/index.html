Job Market Paper

Spatial Choices, Fertility Decisions, and Educational Competition: Insights from Localizing Admission Competition

Abstract

I study how localizing university admission competition affects fertility, private education investment, and residential sorting. I develop a quantitative spatial overlapping-generations model in which households jointly choose location, fertility, and education spending. A Regional Admission Quota System relaxes rural competition but intensifies urban competition, producing opposite regional quantity–quality responses. Although migration toward rural areas partly offsets these responses, aggregate fertility falls and private education spending rises, while the urban–rural human-capital gap narrows substantially. Admission policy therefore also operates as spatial policy.

Working Paper

From Demographic Dividend to Demographic Drag: Economic Growth and Fertility in South Korea

with Wookun Kim and Michael Sposi

Draft (PDF) ↗

Abstract

South Korea experienced an exceptional decline in fertility, from among the highest rates in the world to one of the lowest today. The decline coincided with an aggressive government family-planning campaign and overlapped with Korea’s remarkable economic growth. We study the long-run consequences of this demographic transition using a calibrated overlapping-generations model in which government policy shifts fertility norms, while fertility affects human-capital investment, labor supply, and child-rearing costs. Lower fertility initially creates a demographic dividend by reducing dependency burdens and increasing investment per child. These forces also reshape social norms and future labor supply, allowing low fertility to persist after the policy ends. Over time, however, smaller working-age cohorts increase the burden of supporting a largely elderly population. We quantify the historical gains from Korea’s demographic window and compare them with the emerging and projected costs of demographic drag.
